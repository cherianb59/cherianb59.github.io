## Approaching a post-scarcity world

Walking thorugh the inner west, the streets are constantly adorned with household appliances that are too large for the wheelie bins. Bed frames, TVs, .

FB marketplace has a lot of great deals, and anything that might require a little bit of fixing is very cheap. I bought an ender 3 S1 for $135 which had a worn out nozzle, it was selling for $250 new. You start to realize how much value people discard just because it’s slightly inconvenient.

Most large furniture that doesn’t fit in a car ends up nearly free. If you can borrow a ute or even rent a GoGet van, you can furnish a house for under $500. There’s so much stuff that ownership becomes less about access to resources and more about willingness to deal with logistics.

People eat too much food. Starvation isn’t a visible problem in Australia; malnutrition affects some, but famine doesn’t exist. Walk into any supermarket, and shelves are overflowing with calories in every possible form — wrapped, preserved, frozen, or ready-to-eat.

The price of food, especially if you measure it as calories per hour worked, has dropped massively. Chicken drumsticks are cheaper per kilo than potatoes or dried beans. For the average person, eating enough isn’t the challenge — eating well is. Obesity is now the visible symptom of abundance.

Food waste mirrors the furniture problem: cafés, restaurants, and supermarkets throw away enough to feed small towns. Access, not scarcity, is the issue.

### Bullshit jobs

As David Graeber argued, many people today have bullshit jobs — roles that exist mainly to maintain appearances, fill spreadsheets, or produce slide decks that no one reads.

We’ve automated the hard parts of work, but instead of freeing people, we invented new layers of “coordination,” “strategy,” and “alignment.” You meet people who can’t explain what they do, except that it involves “stakeholder management” and “cross-functional collaboration.”

In a strange way, this is also a symptom of post-scarcity. We’ve solved many material needs, but our economic systems require everyone to stay busy. Productivity has become performance art.

### Bullshit products and services 

The same logic applies to products. Entire industries are built around making us feel like we don’t have enough — that this year’s model is essential, that last year’s is obsolete.

You can buy an electric toothbrush that connects to Wi-Fi, or a fridge that tweets. Subscription boxes for things no one needs. Streaming services selling the same shows with different logos.

The world is full of over-engineered solutions to non-problems, while the basic tools of living — food, shelter, furniture, community — have never been more accessible or abundant.
