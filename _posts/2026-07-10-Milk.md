# Dollar-a-Litre Milk

On 26 January 2011, Coles in Australia cut home-brand milk to $1 per litre, half the price of branded milk (Source: [Senate Economics Committee Inquiry Report](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Senate/Economics/Completed%20inquiries/2010-13/dairyindustrysupermarket2011/report/~/media/wopapub/senate/committee/economics_ctte/completed_inquiries/2010-13/dairy_industry_supermarket_2011/report/c01.ashx)). Woolworths and Aldi followed suit. This was initially seen as a victory for the consumer, lowering prices and helping ease the cost of living. 

However, nine months later in September 2011, farmers complained that because Coles and Woolworths had dropped their prices, they were not getting paid as much ([ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2011-09-13/farmer-anger-spills-over-milk-war-prices/2897108)). Farmers urged consumers to stop buying "private-label" milk (Coles, Woolworths, and Aldi home brands) and instead buy premium branded milk ([Milkmaid Marian](https://milkmaidmarian.com/2012/04/17/ethical-milk-which-brand-to-buy/)).

This unrest led to a parliamentary inquiry into the dairy industry ([Senate Inquiry Index](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Senate/Economics/Completed%20inquiries/2010-13/dairyindustrysupermarket2011/report/index)). A key finding was detailed in a chart showing the [milk revenue share](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Senate/Economics/Completed%20inquiries/2010-13/dairyindustrysupermarket2011/report/~/media/wopapub/senate/committee/economics_ctte/completed_inquiries/2010-13/dairy_industry_supermarket_2011/report/c05.ashx), which broke down where the money from each litre of milk actually went.

The data showed that farmers received the same amount of money per litre from dollar-a-litre milk as they did from branded milk. So, if farmers were getting paid the same, who was taking the financial hit? It was the processors and the retailers. The public had been taken for a ride, media reports urging them to buy branded milk to support farmers were actually helping processors and retailers line their pockets.

When you think about it, this makes sense. Farmers sell their milk to processors, and processors sell the packaged milk to retailers (such as Coles, Woolworths, Aldi, and IGA). Because farmers sell their raw milk in bulk to the processors rather than to individual retail lines, the price they receive does not change based on whether their milk ends up in a branded or private-label bottle. While the public narrative suggested that farmers were being hit by the retail price cuts, the reality was that Woolworths and Coles were absorbing a significant hit to their own margins by selling private-label milk at a loss.

### But what about the processors

Murray Goulburn was an agricultural co-operative formed in 1950 by a group of dairy farmers. It grew to become Australia's largest milk processor and created the Devondale brand.

For nearly 70 years, Murray Goulburn (MG) was the undisputed cornerstone of the Australian dairy industry, operating as the traditional price-setter for the entire Southern Milk Region (the dairy regions of Victoria, South Australia and southern New South Wales). As a cooperative, it was  governed by its farmer-suppliers and operated with the singular mandate to maximize farmgate returns. However, under the leadership of Managing Director Gary Helou, who was appointed in 2011, MG embarked on a highly aggressive, debt-fueled strategy of corporate expansion, capacity building, and a relentless pursuit of global export dominance. Helou championed a strategy of locking the cooperative into skinny margins with major retailers—including a decade-long private label milk contract with Coles—under the rationale that securing massive domestic volume would eventually spur high-margin growth in premium branded products and Asian export markets

The Farmgate Milk Price (FMP) is a weighted average price that Murray Goulburn pays to dairy farmers from whom it acquires milk. Before the start of each financial year, Murray Goulburn announces an ‘opening price’ FMP, which is the average FMP that it will pay to farmers from the start of the financial year.  The opening FMP is expected to be the average minimum price farmers will receive during the financial year, and it is set at a conservative amount to facilitate increases, known as ‘step-ups’, throughout the year.

At the start of a financial year, MG also announces its ‘forecast Final FMP’, which is the average FMP that it expects to pay to farmers for milk acquired from them that financial year, and which is calculated at the end of that financial year. Throughout the financial year, the forecast Final FMP is updated, typically by way of ‘step-ups’.

Murray Goulburn exclusively sets and revises its opening and forecast Final FMP for the season.
[https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/accc-takes-action-against-murray-goulburn]

### 2016 milk clawback

It began at the end of April when Australia's biggest milk processor, Murray Goulburn, slashed the price dairy farmers had been expecting for their milk.

Instead of the $6 a kilogram that farmers had been relying for their milk solids, they were suddenly told they would be getting well under $5 a kilogram. To add insult to injury, the farmers themselves would have to pay back the difference, a total of around $200 million between about 2,500 farmers. It meant many dairy farmers were being paid less for their milk than it cost them to produce it. Sending prize dairy cows to the saleyards for slaughter was the only way many farmers can pay their bills.
[https://www.abc.net.au/news/2016-08-15/dairy-farmers-forced-to-sell-up-in-face-of-plunging-milk-prices/7730638]

Goulburns main rival, Fonterra initially sat on its hands, apparently caught by surprise like the rest of the dairy farmers. Then announced a slashing of the milk price from $5.60 to $1.91kg Milk Solids (MS) – the equivalent to 14 or 15 cents per litre. Fonterra gave no notice, it revised the price for May and June on May 5. There was no time for farmers to plan and they were all faced with a frenzy of late-night nightmarish decision making. [https://milkmaidmarian.com/2017/04/28/accc-takes-helou-hingle-and-mg-to-court-but-lets-fonterra-off-the-hook/]

Many dairy farmers left Murray Goulburn and this instituted the decline of the cooperative. In 2018, following severe financial difficulties and disputes with suppliers over sustainable pricing, its business assets were sold to Saputo Inc., a publicly listed Canadian dairy company. The trading name was later changed to AG Warehouse, and the co-operative was officially placed into liquidation in 2020. The downfall of the co-operative caused significant fury among farmers, particularly regarding the lenient penalties handed down to its former leadership ([eDairyNews: Farmer fury over lenient fine](https://history.edairynews.com/australia/farmer-fury-over-lenient-fine-handed-to-former-murray-goulburn-chief-gary-helou/)).


### ACCC Milk Report 

In 2018, the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) published its comprehensive [Dairy Inquiry Final Report](https://www.accc.gov.au/system/files/1395_Dairy%20inquiry%20final%20report.pdf), shedding further light on these industry dynamics. Regarding the cut farmers got from private label milk and branded milk, it showed the same thing as Senate Inquiry, farmers were getting the same amount.

The ACCC inquiry identified that processors, not the supermarkets, were the ones maintaining and benefiting from opaque pricing models. Processors holding lucrative, high-volume contracts for private-label milk chose to offset their lower supermarket margins by extracting significantly higher returns from branded white milk sales and value-added dairy products like yoghurt, butter, and ice cream.

Furthermore, processors like National Foods correctly argued that consumer demand for milk is relatively inelastic: price drops do not prompt households to buy more milk overall, but simply shift consumer spending from branded to generic options.

The most damning revelation from the ACCC inquiry was the existence of explicit "pass-through" clauses in the confidential supply contracts between major dairy processors and supermarkets. The public had been led to believe that supermarkets used their massive market power to force processors to take losses on $1 milk, forcing processors to slash farmgate prices to survive. 

The ACCC found the exact opposite. Almost all contracts for the supply of private-label milk explicitly allowed processors to pass through any upward movements in farmgate prices directly to the supermarkets. As ACCC Chair Rod Sims unequivocally stated:

> "Almost all contracts between processors and supermarkets for the supply of private label milk allow processors to pass-through movements in farmgate prices to supermarkets. This means processors set their farmgate prices independent of the supermarkets' retail prices."

If a processor determined that they needed to pay farmers more to secure a viable, sustainable milk supply, they had the contractual right to raise the wholesale price charged to the supermarket, and the supermarket was contractually obligated to absorb that increase. Processors were wholly insulated from retail price drops. Therefore, when processors claimed to drought-stricken farmers in New South Wales and Queensland that they could not increase farmgate prices because of the supermarkets' $1 milk strategy, they were engaging in demonstrably false and misleading conduct ([ACCC Media Release](https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/processors-warned-not-to-mislead-dairy-farmers-about-milk-prices)).

### Market Power

A key insight from hte report is the power dynamics between the three main players in the dairy industry, suprmarkets, processors and dairy farmers.

#### Supermarkets vs. Processors

Supermarkets are the dominant dairy retailers in Australia, selling approximately 60 percent of the nation's dairy products annually. All major Australian dairy processors supply supermarkets, though some rely on this channel more than others. Supermarkets represent a critical sales channel for highly perishable dairy products that cannot be exported, such as fresh drinking milk. Consequently, supermarket bargaining power is exceptionally strong, especially when negotiating contracts for private-label products, where consumers' preference for low prices outweighs the value placed on brand names.

#### Processors vs. Farmers

Farmers, by contrast, hold very little bargaining power. Processors typically have the option to purchase milk from hundreds or even more than a thousand different farmers, whose products are largely generic and interchangeable. In contrast, the average farmer has few alternatives, and in some regions only one option for selling their milk. This severe imbalance means that genuine negotiation rarely occurs, and:

* Farmers act as "price takers," forced to accept "take-it-or-leave-it" offers.
* Supply contracts are heavily weighted in favour of processors.
* Processors often fail to provide transparent communication and market information.

The ACCC helped correct this by instituing a code of conduct the farmers and processors are bound by. THe key features are that all milk supply agreements must clearly specify the minimum price payable for milk , limiting the circumstances in which a processor can lower or ‘step down’ the price for milk, and prohibiting the use of any loyalty payments that are conditional on the farmer, supplying milk to the processor after the agreement ends, agreeing to extend the supply period under the current agreement, or entering into a new milk supply agreement with the processor.
